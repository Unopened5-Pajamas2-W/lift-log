## Claims

### Flexible training templates (Walts 2021 + prior literature)
- Study: 32 DIII lacrosse players (15M/17F), 8 weeks (two 4-week blocks), 3 sessions/wk, 22 sessions; flexible group picked daily from 4 session types (green: high-volume or high-intensity; yellow: low-volume or low-intensity; red = skip) vs fixed order (yellow-low-intensity → green-high-volume → yellow-low-volume → green-high-intensity). ~60-min sessions; plus 2×/wk 60-min speed/agility. Result: no between-group differences (VJ +3.9% flex vs +6.4% fixed; agility +0.8% vs +1.6%).
- Prior evidence: Colquhoun 2017 (trained lifters, 9 wks, fixed hypertrophy/power/strength order, last sets to failure on hypertrophy+strength days): equal strength but flexible group had lower dropout (12.5% vs 31%) and fewer missed sessions (4 vs 8). McNamara & Stearne 2010 (12 wks, 2×/wk, all sets to failure, 20/15/10RM rotation vs choose-any-order; each type done 8×): leg press gains ~3× in flexible group; only 1 of 7 strength tests across 3 studies favored flexibility.
- Decision rules: use flexible templates only when (1) training is extremely demanding, (2) life schedule is exhaustive, (3) travel/inconsistent gym access. If chronically fatigued, flexibility is not the fix — cut volume or proximity to failure / rearrange the week.
- Default weekly order (3×/wk muscle frequency): moderate-RPE Mon, low-RPE Wed, high-RPE Fri; or hypertrophy/high-volume Mon → power/low-volume Wed → strength/moderate Fri (power session in middle gives 48-h priming, more strength-day volume).
- Within-month flexibility: 6 high + 6 moderate + 6 low RPE days/month in any order; under stress, scale to 4 high / 8 moderate / 12 low (24 sessions).
- Preferred model: fixed weekly order + a permanent "flex option" slot (low-volume power day or low-RPE assistance-only day) to insert when readiness is low; then resume the schedule. Miss 1 day → push everything back one day; miss 2 days → repeat the week.
- Readiness metrics: HRV failed to improve strength gains or track recovery; well-being scales unproven for acute strength. Best-supported: vertical jump (Watkins 2017: 4 squat sets to failure @80% 1RM, retest 48 h later — VJ drop correlated with fewer reps, r=0.65); use a cutoff (e.g., 1.5 cm drop) → pick easier session. PRS 0–10 scale: a "7" day vs "8–10" probably doesn't need session change — use RPE/velocity intra-session. If truly trashed: take the day off or insert light/power day.
- Asides: exercise-pool choice ("back assistance: your choice") may aid strength (Rauch 2020); stretches >100 s acutely cut force (Fowles 2000), <10 s is risk-free.
- Evidence: RCT (Walts 2021) + prior RCTs (Colquhoun 2017; McNamara & Stearne 2010).

### Proximity to failure & velocity loss (Andersen 2021 + 13-study table)
- Study: 10 trained subjects (7F/3M), unilateral leg press + leg extension 2×/wk × 9 weeks, within-subject: one leg stopped at 15% velocity loss, other at 30%. Week-1 loads: leg press 80% 1RM, extension 75% 1RM; target 5–7 reps (15% leg) vs 12–14 reps (30% leg); +2.5–5 kg next set if reps exceed range top; 2.5-min rests; volume equated (double sets for low-VL leg; start-leg rotates weekly). Actual VL 20.2±6.2% vs 36.3±3.3%; estimated RIR ~5–10 vs ~1–4.
- Result: no differences in strength or muscle-thickness gains; RFD, velocity at same %1RM, pennation, fascicle length unchanged; power output decreased at all loads in both groups. (Rectus femoris ES 0.20 favoring 15% leg, p=0.421 — inconclusive; article critiques missing equivalence testing.)
- Strength rules: at 50–85% 1RM, a 15–30% VL window (~3–5+ RIR) beats ≥40% VL (≈ failure-ish) for 1RM: 10%, 15%, 20%, 25% VL groups each out-gained ≥40% VL groups. Cannot extend to >85% 1RM: a single @90% 1RM is already ~2 RIR for most — reserve 0–2 RIR for high-intensity, low-volume work; keep volume work farther from failure. Peak intensity matters more than average intensity for strength.
- Hypertrophy rules: ≥5 RIR appears sufficient — 4 of 5 VL hypertrophy studies show no close-vs-far difference (one favored close: Martinez-Canton). Caveats: findings are exercise-specific (squat/bench may not transfer to curls); synergist effects unknown (bench @5 RIR ≠ same triceps stimulus); short-ROM assistance fatigue doesn't persist; don't avoid failure always — integrate it selectively (failure work + non-failure volume, chosen carefully).
- Key numbers: Galiano 2020: squat 1RM +10.7% training at 10% VL @50% 1RM (>15 RIR); huge individual variability (Rodriguez-Rosell ranges: +6.4–58.6% at 10% VL, +4.5–66.2% at 30%, +1.8–52.1% at 40%); Klemp 2016 squat 1RM −3.5% to +18.6% (mean +9.4±5.3%). Andersen study averaged ~40% strength gain. 12 of 13 VL studies were not volume-equated; the 40%-VL groups often did ~double the reps per set at same %1RM — confounding "failure is worse" claims.
- Counterexample: Kilgallon 2021 — 3 weeks, floor press @85% 1RM: failure beat 20% VL; failure group did 2.8–3.3 reps/set vs 1.1–1.3 (≈2.5× more reps = practice effect), non-maximal intent velocity prescribed (opposite of other studies), short ROM. Suggests close-to-failure may matter more for short-ROM movements.
- Velocity-loss mechanics: %VL is exercise- and set-dependent; first-rep velocity drifts across sets (0.70→0.60 m/s @70% 1RM), so the same %VL lands at different RIR. Velocity at a given RIR is stable across loads — better method: do one set to failure ~75% 1RM to calibrate your RIR-velocity relationship, then stop sets at an absolute target velocity rather than %VL.
- Explosive adaptations (jump/RFD): keep sets at ≤20% VL.
- Takeaway: non-failure training (a few reps shy, possibly ≥5 RIR) is sufficient — and at moderate intensity likely superior for 1RM; failure is a selective tool.
- Evidence: within-subject RCT (Andersen 2021, JSCR) + 13-study VL compilation (mostly one lab's Smith-machine squat series; 12/13 not volume-equated — author's own critique).

### Attentional focus (Grgic 2021 meta-analysis)
- External focus (outcome/effect of movement — "push the floor away") vs internal (body parts/muscles).
- Acute strength: external focus wins, SMD = 0.34, p<0.001 (7 studies), including big lifts (IMTP, squat/deadlift), not just handgrip/finger tasks.
- Longitudinal strength: external focus favored, SMD = 0.32 (p=0.11) overall; lower-body sub-analysis SMD = 0.47 (p=0.02) — but only 3 longitudinal studies, so tentative.
- Practical: cue externally when strength is the goal; internal focus may instead aid hypertrophy per prior MASS coverage.

### Concurrent training / interference (Schumann 2021 meta-analysis)
- 43 studies, 1,090 subjects, ≥4 weeks, identical RT ± added aerobic work. Results: max strength SMD = −0.06 (p=0.45, nil); hypertrophy SMD = −0.01 (p=0.92, nil); explosive strength SMD = −0.28 (p=0.007, real small negative).
- Subanalyses: modality (running vs cycling), endurance frequency, training status, age, same-session vs separate sessions — none modified the strength effect; total endurance duration not analyzed.
- Decision rules: interference is now a niche concern, not a default. Only be careful when: (1) recovery is compromised (poor sleep, high stress, calorie deficit), (2) endurance training already near your recovery limit, (3) lifting volume already near your limit and adding lots of endurance, (4) goals are explosive/power (jumping, throwing — then limit endurance). Otherwise ~80% of people can combine both.
- Separate modalities when possible (separating by 24 h minimizes interference); heavy endurance volume still implicitly crowds out max hypertrophy/strength volume because recovery is finite.
- History arc: Hickson 1980 (large interference) → Wilson 2012 meta (moderate) → Petré 2021 (no interference for untrained; none for trained with split sessions) → Schumann 2021 (essentially nil for strength/hypertrophy).

## Evidence quality

- Strong and current for its 2022 vantage: JSCR, Sports Medicine, Scand J Med Sci Sports, 2016–2021. Notable: Andersen et al. 2021 (JSCR, volume-equated within-subjects VL trial); Schumann et al. 2021 (Sports Med meta, 43 studies/1,090 subjects); Grgic et al. 2021 (attentional focus); Pareja-Blanco/Rodríguez-Rosell Spanish VL series (≈10 Smith-machine squat trials). Weak spots acknowledged: one meta was a pre-print; longitudinal attentional-focus base is 3 studies; VL literature 12/13 not volume-equated, one lab.

## Corpus-internal flags

- "≥5 RIR maximizes hypertrophy": more aggressive than the "0–3 RIR" norm; author's own caveats (synergists, short-ROM moves, isolation) bound it.
- Velocity-loss thresholds (15–30%) rest on Smith-machine squat studies at 50–85% 1RM, mostly not volume-equated; author distrusts %VL as a prescription tool — his alternative (absolute velocity targets) is the better-specified method.
- Attentional focus longitudinal effect (SMD 0.32–0.47): 3 studies — preliminary.
- Kilgallon counterexample: failure > 20% VL at 85% 1RM — the "train far from failure" rule is intensity- and exercise-bounded.
- Interference de-escalation matches modern view; residual "explosive strength suffers (SMD −0.28)" stands.

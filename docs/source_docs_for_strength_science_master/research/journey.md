## Claims

### Overarching framework
- Strength potential = four factors: (1) big muscles, (2) mastery of the lifts, (3) healthy joints/connective tissue, (4) age. All factors peak in late teens; neural drive declines from the 30s, tendon elasticity from the 50s, neural plasticity from birth.
- Each training phase steered by: "What obstacles are hindering me the most right now?"
- Phase-defining limiters — New: buy-in, movement proficiency, body/muscular awareness, work capacity. Intermediate: muscle mass, trainability/recovery. Advanced: proficiency→mastery, joint health, competition skill.
- Stages are defined by limiting factors, NOT by strength standards.

### New lifter — proficiency & practice
- Purposeful practice: pre-set visualization, one cue per set, post-set analysis, video review (camera side-on between knee and hip height for squat, hip height for deadlift, 6–8 in above bench height for bench).
- Load band: 60–80% 1RM (true beginners: heaviest fully-comfortable weight, down to ~15% lighter).
- Stay ≥3 reps from failure (RIR ≥3), stop the moment form changes — multiple sets of low reps.
- Train each lift 2–4×/week: practice frequency plus muscle protein synthesis window (36–48 h elevated in novices; 12–24 h or less in experienced lifters).
- Kinesthetic awareness via bodyweight work: pick 2–3 movements per session (push-up variants, dips, rows, planks, carries, single-leg/arm work, L-sits), 2 sets to controlled max. Muscular awareness via isolation ("bodybuilding") moves to enable internal cues and weak-link diagnosis.
- Work capacity: men >20% BF / women >30% should cut to 12–15% / 20–25%; aerobic base sufficient if resting HR is high-50s/low-60s (<200 lb) or mid-60s (>200 lb); protein 0.82 g/lb (1.8 g/kg); cutting novices can still gain muscle at ~1% bodyweight/week deficit.
- Phase exit rule (beginner→intermediate): beginner gains mostly neural; when you can no longer add weight week to week without grinding reps, switch — typically after 2–6 months. Per-lift transitions allowed (switch bench training while squat/DL still climb). First-timer muscle +10–20% while squat "skyrockets into the 300s" from neural gains.

### What determines how much you lift (6 factors)
- Muscle size; fiber types; segment lengths; motor learning; motivation/arousal/fatigue; muscle origins/insertions.
- Fiber type: max force per cross-sectional area differs ≤10% between types; a 70% Type-II vs 70% Type-I outlier differs only ~4%; power output peaks at 30–60% 1RM (low at maximal loads), so fiber type barely matters for powerlifting; elite powerlifters (avg squat/deadlift ~285 kg/630 lb, bench ~170 kg/375 lb) have novice-like type I/II ratios (Fry 2003).
- Segment lengths: usually <10% variability; one lift's disadvantage is another's advantage; Wilks normalizes height.
- Origins/insertions matter more than segment lengths: e.g., a 1-inch-lower hamstring origin (vs ~3 in average) = ~33% more hip-extension torque; muscles attach only 2–4 in from joints.
- Muscle size is the only major factor you can change; elite performance correlates with FFM per unit height. (Brechue & Abe 2002: FFM correlations r = 0.86–0.95 with squat/bench/deadlift; Lovera & Keogh 2015: successful powerlifters = more muscle mass per unit height, similar segment lengths.)
- "Weight classes are height classes in disguise" — strategic framing of mass gain vs leverage.

### Intermediate implementation (core protocol)
- Goal: highest weight class possible while lean (<15% BF men / <25% women).
- Main lifts at 75–85% of max; very little below 70% or above 90% except meet peaking.
- Add main-lift variations (paused/front squat, close-grip/pin bench, deficit or opposite-stance deadlift) to push hard without overuse.
- Main-lift volume low-to-moderate; always ≥1–2 reps shy of failure (technical failure).
- Bulk of volume from accessories: sets of 6–15 reps, each muscle/movement 2–3×/week, 4–6 sets (40–70 total reps) per session — chosen over light barbell work to protect joints and not "water down" main-lift motor learning.
- PR cadence: 4-week blocks (small PRs every 4 wks) → when inconsistent, 8-week cycles → then 12-week; you should still PR every 12 weeks during bulk phases for the whole intermediate period.
- Periodization matters little for hypertrophy; vary training for freshness.
- Bulk/cut: gain ~0.5 lb/week until 20–22% BF (men) / 28–30% (women), then cut at 1% BW/week to 10–15% / 20–25%.
- Keep peaking short: a simple 3–4-week peak suffices. Rationale: bodybuilding-style training has the lowest injury rate of strength sports; the best volume-equated powerlifting-style vs bodybuilding-style study (unnamed) found similar hypertrophy but more aches/fatigue from heavier training.
- Phase length 3–8 years; exit when yearly muscle gain drops below 3–5 lb (at similar BF%).

### Advanced
- Remaining gains: coordination + rate coding. Coordination trainable at 80–85% 1RM; rate coding only engages above ~80–85% of maximal force, so mastery needs heavier loads.
- Offseason: high frequency, high volume, relatively low intensity, high variation via main-lift variants; accessories target specific muscular weaknesses; cut generic bodybuilding volume (high main-lift + high accessory volume can't coexist productively).
- Meet prep: decrease frequency/volume to raise intensity; last ~6 weeks — very conservative with accessories; last 4–6 weeks — maximize high-quality lifts at 85–95% 1RM with minimal fatigue (hyper-specific, fresh practice).
- Compete ~2×/year. Progress can continue until injury or aging intervenes.

### Recovery/management
- Banister fitness-fatigue (impulse-response) model; Yerkes-Dodson arousal curve.
- Fat mass lowers testosterone/insulin sensitivity and raises inflammation, hurting training response.
- Sleep/stress are recovery multipliers (depth in filtered-out chapters).

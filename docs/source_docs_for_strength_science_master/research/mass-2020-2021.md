## Claims

### Proximity to failure (Santanielo 2020 review)
- Study: 14 trained men, 10 weeks, within-subjects: unilateral leg press + leg extension 2×/week; one leg to failure at 75% 1RM, other stopped by perceived fatigue (averaged ~1.5–1.6 RIR). 1RM re-tested at week 5; loads reset to 75% of new 1RM. Set prescription = habitual weekly quad sets +20%, split 50/50 → 11.5±5.1 sets/wk leg press, 11.6±5.2 leg extension (range 4–25).
- Failure leg did 13.6% more reps/set (12.0±2.1 vs 10.4±2.8) and 11.5% more volume.
- Outcome: all measures improved both legs, no significant differences; quad CSA +13.5% (failure) vs +18.1% (non-failure) → 4.6% faster growth, ES 0.27; leg press 1RM +4.4% faster non-failure, ES 0.18 (trivial after small-sample correction); pennation angle ES 0.46 favoring non-failure.
- Core decision rule: training 1–2 RIR shy of failure is at least as good as failure for hypertrophy and likely better for strength. Trained lifters on high-frequency programs can take most compound sets to ~4–5 RIR (possibly farther) and still maximize hypertrophy; if hitting a muscle 3×/week or running high volume, 3–5 RIR on main lifts mitigates carryover fatigue. 5 RIR framed as a floor estimate, not a ceiling claim.
- RIR stratification across the week (sample Mon/Wed/Fri legs-back-biceps week): early-week/high-volume sessions — main lifts and "damaging" exercises (flys, RDLs, skull crushers) kept farther from failure; assistance (curls, lateral raises, leg extensions) at 1–2 RIR, last few sets to failure acceptable. Late in the week — last set of a main lift to failure, all assistance 0–2 RIR. Failure/RPE targets not all-or-none; lifter buy-in/enjoyment justifies strategically placed failure work.
- RIR accuracy data (Zourdos 2019 acute study): one set to failure at 70% 1RM squat; lifters predicting 5/3/1 RIR were under by ~5/3.5/2 reps (actual ~10/~6.5/3 RIR). Other data: intra-set RIR predictable to within 1 rep after the first set (Hackett; Mansfield). Helms thesis: 8 weeks, squat+bench 3×/wk, 4–5 RIR ≈ 2–3 RIR for growth (lateral quad +6.6% vs +4.88% in Klemp's closer-to-failure study).
- Strength-specific loading rules: maximizing 1RM requires working up to ≥90% 1RM; a single at 90% is already ~2 RIR; competition prep may include heavy singles at 0–1 RIR (RPE 9–9.5). Avoid failure during volume work; intensity work naturally approaches failure. Both volume and intensity drive strength.
- Velocity-loss proxies (strongest strength evidence cited): Pareja-Blanco — stopping squat sets at 20% velocity loss → +4.6% greater squat 1RM vs 40% loss (≈ failure on >50% of sets), 8 weeks. Sánchez-Moreno — pull-ups, 25% vs 50% velocity loss → ~5% greater strength gains with 25%, 8 weeks.
- Counter-evidence weighed and discounted in-article: Goto 2005 — leg extensions 5×10 to failure (+12.9% quad) vs same sets with 30-s intra-set rest after 5th rep (+4.0%) over 12 weeks — discounted as extended-cluster artifact. Karsten — 4×10RM produced ~5% faster quad growth than 8×5 at 10RM load (≈5 RIR→3 RIR), but once-weekly muscle frequency lowers its weight.
- Frequency: 2–3×/week superior to 1×/week for strength (Grgic 2018 meta); possibly preferable for hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2016/2019 metas).
- Proposed research design (in-article): four groups at failure / 1–3 RIR / 4–6 RIR / 7–10 RIR with damage markers to 48 h, session RPE, 12-week adherence follow-up.
- Evidence: within-subject RCT (Santanielo 2020); supporting: Davies et al. 2016 (Sports Med) failure-vs-non-failure meta (with erratum); Grgic et al. 2018 (Sports Med) frequency-strength meta; Schoenfeld frequency metas (2016, 2019). Weaknesses flagged: small samples, no equivalence testing, self-reported RIR.

### Concurrent training / interference (Kilen 2020 review)
- Study: 290 Danish military conscripts (285 M/6 F; 87-person subsample biopsied), 9 weeks, 2 h/week study training on top of ~20 h/week military marching/standing (0–10 kg loads). Three groups: micro (eight 15-min sessions/wk — 4 running + 4 lifting, ≥2 h apart, ≤3/day); classic (30 min running then 30 min lifting, 2×/wk); standard (two 60-min mixed sessions/wk, ~40% lifting / ~60% running + muscular endurance, uncontrolled). Running progressed moderate → intervals. Lifting volume = 6 sets/week × 5 reps (minimal).
- Outcome: pull-ups rose in micro & classic (both p<0.05 vs standard); knee-extensor MVC rose in all groups (no group differences); only micro improved elbow-flexor strength; grip unchanged. Biopsy: type II fiber area +12.81% (combined); satellite cells per type II fiber +33.33% (combined +22.22%); micro group type IIa proportion +5.1%; combined type I distribution −3.7%.
- Interference decision rules: magnitude is dose- and mode-dependent — studies that show it are designed to cause it (running 1 h/day 4–6 days/wk = pronounced; cycling 3×30 min = negligible). Lower-body lifting volume drops ~25% when lifting 4 h after aerobic work, still impaired at 8 h (Sporer & Wenger 2003). Running interferes more than cycling (Wilson 2012 meta). Interference effect "all but disappears when a caloric deficit is avoided" (Murach & Bagley). Hickson 1980 precedent: −0.8 kg body mass yet +25% squat 1RM in 10 weeks.
- Practical protocol: if cardio and lifting must be adjacent, lift first. Use 15-minute lifting micro-sessions spread through the week to keep main lifts away from cardio; put squats/deadlifts on non-cardio days or immediately before aerobic work; do only assistance work after cardio.
- Cardio mode guidance: HIIT/sprinting is mechanistically lifting-like but impractical — sprint muscle damage lasts 48–72 h; a meta (Sabag 2018) shows HIIT can attenuate strength gains; cycling sprints beat running sprints. Steady-state cycling 30 min at 50–60% VO2max, 2×/wk has no measurable negative effect on strength/hypertrophy (Dolan 2015). Flexible template: 12 cardio sessions/month, 6 HIIT + 6 any non-running mode, sprints on high-energy days. Running 1–2×/wk, a few miles, fine if not immediately before lifting or ~24 h before a heavy lower-body day.
- Note: referenced "Table 5" cardio cheat-sheet is not reproduced in the digest text.
- Evidence: large trial (Kilen 2020); supporting: Wilson et al. 2012 interference meta; Sabag et al. 2018 HIIT-concurrent meta; Sporer & Wenger 2003; Hickson 1980. Weaknesses flagged: no lifting-only control group, minimal lifting volume.

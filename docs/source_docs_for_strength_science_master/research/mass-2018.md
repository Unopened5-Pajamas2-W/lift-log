## Claims (atomic)

**Volume → strength (Nuckols on Ralston et al. 2017, Sports Med MA)**
- Bins: LWS <5 sets/exercise/week; MWS 5–9; HWS ≥10; analyses pooled <5 vs ≥5. [§Volume article]
- 9 studies (4 trained, 5 untrained), 8–26 wk, mean 2.8±0.3 sessions/wk, mean 78.2±4.1% 1RM, weekly volumes 1–12 sets. [§Methods]
- ≥5 sets/exercise/week significantly better (p=0.003) but ES trivial–small: ΔES 0.18 overall; 0.18 multi-joint; 0.23 single-joint; 0.14 exercise-specific 1RM (p=0.06). Practically ~20–25% faster strength gains. [§Findings/Interpretation]
- Schoenfeld 2017 hypertrophy meta cross-compare: 10+ sets ES 0.52 vs <5 ES 0.31 (Δ0.21; Δ0.10 after outlier removal) — absolute volume advantage similar for strength and hypertrophy; relative advantage larger for hypertrophy (33–60%). [§Interpretation]
- Low volume still yields solid short-term gains (fine for busy/non-competitors); higher volume matters more as training age rises; data stop at 10 sets/exercise/week — tipping point where excess volume degrades strength gains is unknown; adjust to individual recovery/response. [§Application/Next Steps]

**Squat vs deadlift fatigue (Zourdos on Barnes et al. 2017)**
- 10 trained men (≥2 yrs, 3×/wk; squat 1RM 158 kg, DL 191 kg); crossover 8×2 @95% 1RM, 5-min rest, 1 week apart; measures pre, 5 min, 30 min post. [§Methods]
- No significant between-lift differences in MVIC, EMG, voluntary activation, control stimulus force, or T/C → deadlift does not cause greater acute central/peripheral fatigue than squat. [§Findings]
- Limits: high-intensity only; no 24–96 h recovery time course; only quads measured (posterior chain untested) — weekly total-body deadlift fatigue may still be higher. [§Interpretation]
- Frequency: squat 3×/wk vs deadlift 1×/wk → +9.21% vs +7.14% 1RM over 8 wks (Zourdos DUP study); lower DL frequency viable, not mandatory; raise frequency gradually via repeated bout effect; macro periodization can be identical across lifts while weekly frequency/volume differ; biomechanics, injury history, training age, availability, preference set frequency. [§Application]
- "The best way to improve the deadlift is to deadlift" (Hales 2009) endorsed; some squat→DL crossover exists. [§Application]

**Velocity-based training (Zourdos on Banyard et al. 2017)**
- 18 men (squat ≥1.5×BM; mean 142 kg); baseline 1RM warm-up: 3 reps @20/40/60% + 1 @80/90%. [§Methods]
- ACV & MPV reliable at 20–90% 1RM, NOT at 100% (CV% 19.4%/18.0% at 1RM); peak velocity reliable everywhere. Acceptance criteria ICC >0.70, CV <10%, ES <0.59. [§Findings]
- Equivalent prescriptions (squat): 4×8 @70% = 4×8 @ RPE 5–7 = 4×8 @ 0.40–0.70 m/s; 3×1 @80% = RPE <5 = 0.55–0.75 m/s; 5×3 @85% = RPE 7–9 = 0.30–0.45 m/s (velocity = last rep of set). [§Interpretation]
- Anchors: 1RM @90% ≈ ACV 0.34 m/s ≈ RPE 8–9 → heavy singles 3×1 @ 0.30–0.40 m/s. Cross-study: ACV at 100% 1RM ≈ 0.23–0.26 m/s. [§Interpretation]
- Cooke thesis ACV/RPE ladder (squat, n=58): 30%→1.06 m/s (~RPE 1.5), 40%→1.00 (2), 50%→0.91 (3), 60%→0.82 (4), 70%→0.69 (5), 80%→0.55 (6.5), 90%→0.39 (8.5), 100%→0.26 (9.5). (Unpublished master's thesis — weakest link.) [§Interpretation]
- Warm-up readiness rule (60% ACV; smallest worthwhile change 0.06 m/s): <0.76 m/s & RPE >4 → light day; 0.76–0.88 & RPE 3–4 → moderate; >0.88 & RPE <3 → heavy day; usable for attempt selection on 1RM days. [§Interpretation]
- Progress without maxing: same load moved faster = stronger; velocity gauges block-to-block progress/taper. [§Application]
- VBT + RPE complementary: velocity ignores technique error, RPE/RIR captures it; warm-up RPE @85% may predict daily 1RM better than velocity (3-person case series). [§Application]
- Femur length does NOT predict squat ACV (r=0.02); training age does (longer-trained lift slower 1RM velocities). Helms dissertation: RPE-autoregulated 8-wk prescription beat %1RM prescription for squat/bench gains. [§Application]

## Evidence quality
Structured reviews of named primary studies, full reference lists. Notable: Ralston 2017 (Sports Med volume meta); Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017; Zourdos 2016 (DUP + RIR-RPE validation); Banyard et al. 2017 (load-velocity reliability); Krieger 2009; Morton 2017; Hales 2009. 2016–2017 JSCR/Sports Med base; methodologically literate (Egger's test, I², ICC/CV).

## Unique content
1. Central-fatigue measurement (voluntary activation / control stimulus force) applied to squat vs deadlift. 2. Why long-term deadlift research is scarce (novelty effect, injury risk, time cost). 3. ACV/MPV unreliable at 100% 1RM while peak velocity stays reliable. 4. Velocity–RPE cross-reference ladder + numeric warm-up readiness branching rule. 5. Effect-size pedagogy (trivial ES can be practically large).

## Caveats / superseded-looking
1. "20–25% faster" volume framing — later frequency/volume-equated work suggests strength is less volume-sensitive; article itself hedges (data stop at 10 sets/exercise). 2. Deadlift-fatigue: acute finding solid, weekly recovery unmeasured — unresolved. 3. Acute testosterone/cortisol treated descriptively; field later deprecates acute hormones as adaptation drivers. 4. "Autoregulated RPE beats %1RM" (Helms dissertation) — small sample, non-peer-reviewed venue. 5. RPE-after-warm-up beats velocity for readiness — 3-person case series.

## The Pyramid framework (precise)

Six levels, foundational (largest impact, bottom) → optional (smallest, top). ~80% of progress comes from the bottom four levels; top levels are mostly "how not to do it wrong". Layers less clear-cut than the Nutrition Pyramid because training variables are interdependent.

1. **L1 Adherence** — Realistic, Enjoyable, Flexible ("REF yourself"). Weekly schedule, time frames, life disruptions, missed workouts, concurrent activities, injury management.
2. **L2 Volume & Intensity** — longest chapter, "most important concepts": set volume, load, proximity to failure, frequency. Minimum/maximum/practical effective dose (ED), fractional set counting, RPE/RIR, evidence-based practice.
3. **L3 Progression** — distinct from overload: overload = stimulus above current adaptation; if you can progress, overload occurred. Training status, fitness-fatigue model, deloads/tapers/intro cycles, periodization-as-programming (linearity + variation), Strength Progression System (volume → load → peak), autoregulated double progression, plateau flowchart.
4. **L4 Exercise Selection** — movement specificity and variation for strength (competition lifts + targeted secondary lifts); feasibility (PLATE); exercise order. For competitive strength athletes this rises in importance.
5. **L5 Rest Periods** — straight-set minimums + time-saving techniques (supersets, drop sets, rest-pause).
6. **L6 Lifting Tempo** — eccentric/concentric/transition durations; mostly pitfall-avoidance.

Core prioritization logic: fix big rocks first (adhere → dose → progression → lifts → rest → tempo); adjust via feedback (plateau flowchart, reactive deloads), not absolutes; "optimal" doesn't exist as a fixed destination.

## Claims

### L1 Adherence
- Three conditions: Realistic, Enjoyable, Flexible.
- Missed workouts: just resume where you left off; don't cram doubles in hard phases; 3 consecutive training days produce similar gains to spaced sessions.
- One activity must take priority; for lifters adding cardio: cardio after lifting, or ≥3 h separation, or different days; pick low-impact/eccentric-light modes (bike, swim, elliptical, row); 20–30 min sessions, or keep under 1 h.
- Interference: hypertrophy largely unaffected; strength/power can be blunted.
- Injury rates/1000 h: bodybuilding 0.24–1.0; powerlifting 1.0–4.4; weightlifting 2.4–3.3; CrossFit 3.1; strongman 4.5–6.1; Highland Games 7.5 (basketball 8.5–11.1).
- Red flags → see a professional (radiating pain, bruising, dizziness, nausea). Pain is an unreliable tissue-damage signal (imaging ≠ symptoms in low-back pain). BFR lets limbs train at 20–30% 1RM during joint pain.

### L2 Volume
- Count fractional sets: full set for primary muscles/tested lift; half-set for secondary muscles/assistance lifts.
- Hypertrophy: minimum ED 4 sets/muscle/wk; max ~30; practical 10–20 (efficiency: ≤10 high, 18–19 modest, 30 poor; >30 likely plateau).
- Strength: minimum ED 1 set/lift/wk; ~5 sets/lift/wk maxes short-term benefit (set 2 ≈ doubles rate of gain; benefit plateaus ~5th set); practical 3–5 sets/lift/wk short-term, 5–10 sets/muscle/wk long-term (hypertrophy underpins long-term strength).
- Caveats (authors'): studies avg ~10 weeks, ~40% untrained, low-volume studies were more specific → plateau may be exaggerated for trained lifters.
- Supporting: powerlifters gain 6–12 wks on 3–6 heavy sets/lift/wk of 1–5 reps; 1–3 sets plateau ~2 months; 15,000-lifter 1-set-to-failure dataset plateaued in 1–2 years; Hubal cohort: +54% avg curl 1RM in 12 wks on 18 sets/wk. Latella (9000+ powerlifters): ~10% total increase in first competitive year, then ~9 more years for the next 10%.
- Practical inverted-U: too much volume → deload spiral, injury, burnout even if research shows no downturn.
- Volume-load (sets×reps×load) only meaningful within one person, one lift, similar rep ranges.
- Dose rule: "Do enough to progress, not as much as possible; add volume only when plateaued and everything else is dialed in."

### L2 Intensity
- Load = %1RM; proximity = RPE/RIR (RPE 10 = 0 RIR; 9.5 = could add load; 9 = 1 RIR; 8.5 = 1–2; 8 = 2; 7.5 = 2–3; 7 = 3; 5–6 = 4–6).
- Estimate 1RM from a 2–5RM, not a 1RM test.
- Reps-allowed table: 100%→1; 95%→2–4; 90%→3–6; 85%→5–9; 80%→8–12; 75%→9–15; 70%→11–18 — variance is huge (well-trained squatters: 6–26 reps at 70%) → %1RM is for framing/progress-gauging, NOT prescription; prescribe RPE/RIR.
- RIR accuracy: avg ~1-rep underestimation; worse at higher reps (>12) and higher RIR. Load correction: adjust ~4% per rep off target.
- Failure: momentary failure is the objective definition.
- RIR's independent effect on strength ≈ negligible; load is the strength driver (linear dose-response ≥80% > 60–79% > <60%).
- Hypertrophy sets must be ≥~4–5 reps (too short otherwise) and load >~30% 1RM.
- Strength recommendation: 1–8RM (~80%+ 1RM), ~7–0 RIR as dictated by load; add >90% singles (daily-max data: singles alone gave small gains; +2×3 at 80% of daily max produced meaningful total gains) but most extra volume belongs in hypertrophy work.
- Hypertrophy RIR ladder by exercise class (also Guide Table 7.7): lower free-weight compounds 4–8 reps @ 4–2 RIR; lower machine/upper pressing 4–12 @ 4–1; machine pressing/pulling 6–15 @ 3–0; isolation 8–20 @ 2–0.
- Sequence example: squats/deadlifts 4–6 @ 3–4 RIR or 6–8 @ 2–3; leg press 6–8 @ 2–3 or 8–12 @ 1–2; isolation 8–12/12+ @ 0–2, final sets to failure optional.

### L2 Frequency
- Strength: fractional frequency has dose-response — biggest jump 1→2 sessions/lift/wk (modeled gain rate ~13%→~17%), diminishing returns to 6/wk.
- Recommendation 2–6×/lift/wk, 1–2 direct sets per main lift per session.
- Hypertrophy frequency's independent effect ≈ negligible — volume-distribution tool: never exceed ~11 fractional sets/muscle/session.
- Frequency-by-volume table: 4–10 sets → 1–2×; 11–20 → 2–3×; 21–30 → 3+.
- Skill retention argues ≥2 exposures/lift/wk (one can be light, e.g., 3×1 @80%).

### L3 Training status & overload
- Novice: add load/reps each session/week (phase lasts 6 months–years). Intermediate: reps in 10–20 range week-to-week, or load in low-rep ranges month-to-month (most reach by year 1; may stay through years 4–5). Advanced: a rep or two month-to-month or small load increases over longer timeframes.
- Minimum ED to keep progressing rises with training status, but ideal volume doesn't necessarily skyrocket.

### L3 Fatigue, OTS, deloads
- Performance = fitness − fatigue. True OTS (months-long decline) essentially never demonstrated from lifting alone (Grandou 2020; Fry's 10×daily-1RM-for-2-weeks protocol the only candidate); underfed-lifter "OTS" is really low energy availability.
- Fatigue signs: worse sleep, dreading gym, declining loads/reps, worse aches, more illness.
- Reactive deload trigger: weekly 5-item self-check (dreading gym / sleep worse / loads or reps decreasing / stress worse / aches worse) — 0–1 yes: continue; 2+: deload.
- Standard deload: cut sets ~30–50% across all exercises, keep load/RIR and frequency; targeted deloads for localized regions (drop deadlifts/squats for lumbar soreness); never deload deeper than needed — cessation >3 days can drop strength.
- Taper: cut volume 50–80%, maintain or slightly raise load; expected +2–4% strength; length scaled to fatigue (days to weeks).
- Intro cycle: at the start of a new higher-volume/lower-intensity phase, run ~75% of planned volume slightly further from failure.
- Coleman 2024: taking week 5 completely off in a hard 9-week program gave no benefit and slightly worse strength/soreness → no automatic monthly deloads.
- Plateau volume moves: −20% across the board if chronically under-recovered despite fixes; +20% (global or targeted) only if recovered, technically sound, still stalled (research-supported).

### L3 Periodization & programming
- Periodization = long-term planning; programming = short/mid-term manipulation.
- Moesgaard 2022 meta (35 studies, ~1000 participants): periodized > non-periodized for 1RM regardless of model; undulating > for trained lifters (variation); benefits also likely from specificity timed near the test (linearity).
- Two useful concepts: linearity (high-volume/low-intensity → low-volume/high-intensity) and variation.
- Session ordering matters (Zourdos DUP: placing a light singles day between the high-volume day and the heavy AMRAP day produced bigger 1RM gains).
- Strength programming = appropriate dose/frequency + specificity at the right time + ordering that protects recovery.

### L3 Strength Progression System
- Every session opens with a single at prescribed RPE on each main lift (≤5 lifts) → track estimated 1RM trend (same calculator always).
- Volume phase (6–12 wks): build muscle. 1–3 singles/lift/wk progressing RPE 5→8 (5→2 RIR); secondary lifts 10–20 sets/wk per main-lift muscle group, mostly 6–20 reps @ 0–3 RIR via autoregulated double progression, favoring low-stress non-specific exercises (machines) over high-volume competition lifts. e1RM targets: novice ↑ weekly; intermediate ↑ over phase; advanced maintain.
- Load phase (4–8 wks): 2–4 singles/lift/wk progressing RPE 6→9 (4→1 RIR); after each single, 2 back-off sets of 3–5 reps @ 5–8 RPE (~80–85% of day's e1RM), linearly stepping reps 5→4→3 across the phase; secondary volume halved to 5–10 sets/wk, stepping down 1–2 sets every 1–2 wks. e1RM targets: novice weekly; intermediate every 2–3 wks; advanced over phase. Use close variations (SSB, high-bar, block pulls, medium-grip bench) if main-lift volume isn't tolerable.
- Peak phase (2–4 wks; 2 if volume+load totaled 10–12 wks, 3 if 13–15, 4 if 16+): 2–5 singles/lift/wk at 7–10 RPE; back-off sets shrink 4→2 reps (~85%); secondary 0–4 sets/wk. Final two weeks: w/o −2: sessions ordered SBD ~9–10 RPE → SB ~8–9 → bench-only ~7–8 → SBD opener practice at planned openers (~90–92% 1RM / ~8 RPE), 1×2 back-off after singles. w/o −1: drop bench-only day; singles at 7–8 RPE then 6–7 RPE; final SBD 1–2 days out = primer session (singles at 4–5 RPE only). After peaking: intro week before returning to volume phase.

### L3 Autoregulated double progression
- All hypertrophy work + secondary lifts: pick a rep+RIR range (e.g., 10–15 @ 0–2 RIR); stop at RIR ceiling; same load for remaining sets; if set 1 misses the rep range, correct load ~4% per rep; increase load when set 1 hits rep-range top at RIR-range top; trends over sessions; intermediates may take ~5 sessions per load jump (novices faster, advanced ~2×); jumping the planned increment based on how you feel is encouraged (strength vs rep-endurance recover at different rates).

### L3 Plateau decision rules + flowchart
1. Are you actually plateaued relative to training status?
2. Any "no" on: sleeping 7+ h; not in an energy deficit; protein ≥0.7 g/lb (1.6 g/kg); neither over- nor under-rating RPE; muscles/lifts trained ≥2×/wk (strength); exercise selection/ROM/tempo appropriate and consistent → fix it, rerun a mesocycle.
3. Are you recovering? (0–1 "yes" on the fatigue checklist → likely recovering; 2+ → not.) Not recovering → light week; if fatigue returns quickly, permanently cut volume ~20% or reorganize.
4. Recovering and stalled → increase dose ~20% or fix technique.
5. Joint pain only → switch to 12–20-rep sets, or BFR limbs at 20–30% 1RM @ 0–3 RIR (wrap ~7/10 tightness).

### L4 Exercise selection
- Specificity is multidimensional (movement × load), not a simple spectrum: complex lifts are specificity-sensitive and transfer outward (squat → leg press) but simple lifts don't transfer inward (leg press ↛ squat).
- ~5 fractional sets/lift/wk captures the maximal benefit of specific training; extra specific heavy volume has opportunity cost (González-Badillo: 46 vs 93 vs 184 heavy reps @ 90–100% over 10 wks — no significant difference; moderate group best) — invest extra volume in hypertrophy-support work instead.
- Varied practice (contextual interference, e.g., Fonseca 2014) can beat single-exercise training, especially early/intermediate; variation must stay relevant.
- Secondary lifts targeted at bottlenecks (Helms coaching anecdotes, flagged "not proof"): sumo swap for recoverability; heavy rack holds for grip; minimal-squat + machine volume for a long-femur lifter; paused deadlifts at mid-shin for bar drift.
- Sticking points: pausing at the visible sticking point is logically flawed (braking ≠ force; visible stall follows the force deficit) but pauses help control/position; isometrics at force deficits plausible but hard to locate without lab tools; variations that punish technical faults (front squat for squat-morning); accommodating resistance (bands/chains): no average benefit (2022 meta ~500 participants) but possibly useful near lockout or equipped lifters; explosive/speed work individually responsive (some responders, some not).
- PLATE checklist (feasibility): Painful? Loadable? Available? Time-efficient? Enjoyable?
- Exercise order: strength gains slightly greater when an exercise is performed first (2021 meta, 11 studies); hypertrophy indifferent; multi-joint free weights first; weak-point exception only if it won't hurt the big lifts.

### L5 Rest periods
- Strength main lifts: rest until ready, ≥2 min minimum (Grgic 2018 review), often 3–5+; acceptable set-to-set drift ~1–2 reps/RIR.
- Hypertrophy: ≥90 s average; 90 s floor, more for strong lifters/lower-body compounds.
- Supersets: antagonist-paired and peripheral-paired preserve (sometimes beat) straight-set performance; 2024 RCTs: same hypertrophy in half to two-thirds the time, at ~+1 RPE cost; rest ~2 min between supersets (compound pairs need a 3–5-min cycle; isolation pairs 1–2 min); never pair lower-body compounds with other big lifts.
- Drop sets: load −~20% per drop; ~13 drop sets ≈ 10 straight sets for hypertrophy (Sødal 2023); counting rule: 4 drop sets (incl. initial) = 3 straight sets; biggest time savings.
- Rest-pause: 20-s mini-rests, same load, until target reps (e.g., 30 total); similar adaptations when volume matched (Enes 2021 — rest-pause group gained most strength by doing most reps at the heaviest load); impractical for heavy squats; more fatigue than straight sets.
- Longo 2022: rest structure matters only insofar as it changes total volume.

### L6 Lifting tempo
- Muscles ~40% stronger eccentrically.
- Strength: lower as fast as control allows; lift concentrically with maximal intent; intentionally slow concentrics impair strength gains (Hermes & Fry 2023, 625 participants).
- Hypertrophy: eccentric 1–4 s under muscular control; never let gravity move the load; total rep duration ≤8 s (Schoenfeld 2015 and Moreno-Villanueva 2022: >8 s rep duration = poorer hypertrophy); slow-eccentric 3–6 s can make light loads more stimulative when pain/fatigue limits heavy work.
- Pauses: dissipate elastic energy (acutely weaker) but pin/pause training can build active force in the sticking region (pin squats ↑ 1RM same as back squats); 1–2 s stretch pauses won't meaningfully boost hypertrophy.
- Supramaximal eccentrics: small, non-significant lower-body 1RM edge (Buskard 2018), higher damage/injury risk — not advised without specialist setup.
- Mind-muscle connection: useful only for light/isolation work; internal focus can inhibit heavy-lift strength — use external cues.
- Technique: no universal "correct" form; meet your sport's standards, maximize 1RM, stay pain-free and consistent (consistent tempo/ROM so progress is real).

### Guide to program building
- Step 1: choose days/wk first (2 minimum; most need 3–5; advanced rarely on 2 days). Strength Frequency Matrix (Table 7.1) maps days/wk (2–6) to main-lift weekly frequencies (1.5–6), with combined S/B and B/D sessions; assistance sharing musculature adds 0.5 to a lift's frequency.
- For ~90% of lifters: 3–5 days/wk, muscle/lift frequency 2–4×/wk.
- Hypertrophy volume tiers by time budget: 4–8 sets/muscle (~1–2.5 h/wk) lowest total stimulus; 9–12 (~3–4.5 h); 13–16 (~5–6.5 h); 17–20 (~7–8.5 h); 21–30 (~9+ h, via specialization) highest if sustainable.
- Avoid heavy secondary work for a muscle the day before its main-lift work.
- Main-lift load/RPE has no cheat sheet — dictated by phase and proximity to peak.
- Progression: double progression + phasic approach; phase summary tables; When Progress Plateaus (status table, flowchart, self-assessment).

### WARMING UP
- Purposes: raise temperature, prep tissue/neuromuscular system, small injury-risk reduction.
- Static stretching alone: no independent injury-prevention effect (2025 meta, 15 trials, >9000 participants); pre-training static stretching can blunt strength/power; brief (<60 s), not-to-discomfort static is harmless; static-then-dynamic ordering preserves/increases performance; foam rolling ~60 s modest pressure slightly helps.
- Full-ROM lifting ≈ stretching for ROM (2023 meta, 55 studies, 2756 participants) — warm-up sets with gradually increasing ROM usually suffice.
- Recommended template: optional 5-min general aerobic; 3–5-min dynamic drills (7–10 min if no general warm-up); ascending warm-up sets (1–2-min rests): for ≤6-rep working sets — 5–10 reps ≤40%, 3–5 reps ~60%, 1–3 reps ~80%, 1 rep 90%; for ≥6-rep sets — 5–10 @ 40%, 4–6 @ 60%, 2–4 @ 80%, 1–2 @ 85–90% (optional PAPE, using %1RM of the day).
- PAPE: 1–2 sets of 1–3 reps @ 85–90% 1RM 5–15 min before working sets typically adds ~1–2 reps to failure (range 1–7 when present); the system's daily singles double as PAPE.
- Machines need no warm-up beyond the first exercise for a muscle.

## Corpus-internal flags

- Anti-%1RM prescribing: treats fixed percentage schemes as impractical (huge inter-individual rep variance); prefers RPE/RIR — divergence from classic percentage-based powerlifting programming (and from MASS-20's percent templates); still uses ~80–85% anchors for back-off loads.
- Deload skepticism: argues against calendar-based deloads (Coleman 2024), against "hidden fatigue" narrative, OTS from lifting functionally nonexistent — stronger pushback than much mainstream writing; underlying reviews support it.
- Strength-volume plateau at ~5 sets/lift: rests on a preprint meta-regression, caveated by authors (short studies, untrained majority, specificity confounds); real-world higher-volume practice acknowledged but often framed as unnecessary — the book's most contestable quantitative claim.
- Frequency benefit up to 6×/lift/wk (diminishing) — same preprint; "1→2 is the big jump" is the safe takeaway.
- "RIR negligible for strength": true only load-controlled; stated carefully.
- Hypertrophy ceiling ~30 sets: conservative vs some 2024–2025 discourse; consistent with its own preprint source.
- Taper/deload percentages, ±20% volume moves, ~4%/rep correction: expert-derived, not trial-derived (book says so explicitly).
- Debunked legacy ideas (explicitly): short rests for hypertrophy via "growth hormone"; time-under-tension as independent growth driver; intentionally slow concentrics for strength; pausing exactly at visible sticking point; stretching as mandatory warm-up/injury prevention; "overtraining" from normal lifting; supramaximal eccentrics as clear winner.

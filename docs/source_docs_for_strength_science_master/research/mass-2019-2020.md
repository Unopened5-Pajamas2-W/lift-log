## Claims

### VBT vs percentage-based loading (Dorrell 2019; Nuckols)
- Six weeks, 16 trained men (squat 140.2 kg ≈ 1.5×BW, deadlift 176.6 kg ≈ 2×BW), 2 sessions/week. Two 3-week waves: wave 1 ramps 70% → 85–88% 1RM, wave 2 80–82% → 95%.
- Percentage program (squat/bench/OHP): wk1 3×8@70; wk2 8,6,5@70/75/80; wk3 6,5,3@75/80/85; wk4 8,6,5@70/75/80 (session 2: 70/75/82); wk5 6,5,3@78/85/90 (session 2: 6,4,2@78/88/92); wk6 5,3,2+@85/90/95 (session 2 deload: 4×4@70). Deadlift session-1 wk6 5,3,2+@85/90/95. Accessories at 2 RIR; lunges loaded at 0.6(6RM squat + 14.82 kg).
- VBT group: group velocity zones + velocity stops (set cut at 20% velocity loss; exact interpretation ambiguous); example target: squat @70% = 0.74–0.88 m/s.
- Result: VBT gained ~50% more strength (+37.3 vs +25.1 kg summed across 4 lifts) with 5.9% less volume load (~19% less relative volume); significant between-group effects only for bench and jump height.
- Caveats (stated in article): velocity feedback doubles as external cueing/confound; short study; group (not individualized) targets (individuals differ 0.1–0.2 m/s at equal %1RM).
- Application: without a velocity device, RPE stops and RPE load targets likely work as well.
- Related rule: stopping sets at 20% (not 40%) velocity loss beat higher-volume training in Pareja-Blanco 2017.
- Evidence: RCT (Dorrell 2019, JSCR).

### Specificity vs periodization (Suarez 2019; Zourdos)
- 7-month observational, 9 competitive weightlifters, 4 days/week but 7 sessions (3 two-a-days), three blocks: strength-endurance (SE), strength/power (SP), peaking (1-wk overreach + 3-wk taper). SE: 3×10; SP: 3×5 → 3×3 → 3×2 with 1×5 drop set @60% 1RM; peaking wk1 5×5 → 3×3 → 3×3 → 3×2.
- Intensity zones: VL 65–70%, L 70–75%, ML 75–80%, M 80–85%, MH 85–90%, H 90–95%.
- Adaptations were block-specific (SAID): CSA/muscle thickness up in SE (ES 0.31/0.37), down in SP (MT −0.24); RFD up in SP/peaking, flat/down in SE; nothing ANOVA-significant (well-trained).
- Periodization conclusions: periodized > non-periodized (Williams 2017 meta); Willoughby 1993 classic (16 weeks progressing every 4 weeks 10s@79% → 8s@85% → 6s@88% → 4s@92% beat constant sets).
- Frequent maxing works short-term: daily 1RM squats +10.8/9.5/5.8% in 37 days (220→241 kg), but no long-term data; near-max 2×/week ≈ volume training in novices (Mattocks 2017).
- Hedge: 70% of strength variance explained by muscle size (Trezise 2016) → keep volume blocks; "periodization = long-term trends, programming = today's sets/reps."
- Integrated DUP template (Table 6): Volume block 1 (5–8 wk): squat 3×10@65% (5–7 RPE) → 4×8@70% → 4×6@75%; bench same; deadlift 8×1@75% → 4×1@85%; assistance 3–5 sets, 15–20 → 10–15 → 6–10 reps @5–8 RPE. Volume block 2 (5–8 wk): squat 3×8@72.5% → 3×6@77.5% → 4×4@82.5% (6–8 RPE); deadlift 7×1@80% → 3×1@87.5%; assistance 12–15 → 8–12 → 6–8 reps. Intensity block (3–5 wk): squat/bench 3×5@80% (7–9 RPE) → 3×3@85–87.5% → double@9 RPE; deadlift 3×1@85% → 1×1@87.5% → 3×1@90%; assistance 10–12 → 8–10 → 6–8 @7–9 RPE. Average RPE rises as volume falls.
- Year-round specificity tactics: heavy single @8 RPE (~88–92% 1RM) before volume work ~1×/week; or daily-max template (Table 5): Mon squat single@9–9.5 + 3×8@70%; Wed bench single@9–9.5 + 3×8@70% and dead single@9–10 + 4×5@70%; Fri squat single@9–9.5 + 3×5@77.5%; Sat bench single@9–9.5 + 3×5@77.5% and dead single@9–10 + 4×2@80%.
- Within-week DUP (e.g., 10/8/6 or wider 10/6/2 rep undulation) keeps heavy-ish work present even in volume blocks. DUP > LP head-to-head for strength; periodization unproven for hypertrophy (Grgic 2017).
- Evidence: observational (Suarez 2019) + MAs (Williams 2017) + classics. Authors concede post-test timing may confound periodization-meta benefits.

### RPE/RIR complete guide (Zourdos)
- History: Borg 6–20 (1970, ~heart-rate mapping), CR10 (1982), OMNI (2006), Hackett 2012, Tuchscherer RTS manual (2008, RIR descriptors — practice preceded science), Zourdos 2016 validated RIR-based scale against velocity. Session RPE (Foster 2001): 0–10, taken 30 min post-workout, tracks global fatigue.
- Scale: 10 = no RIR; 9.5 = no RIR but heavier load possible; 9 = 1 RIR; 8.5–8 = 2; 7.5–7 = 3; 5–6 = 4–6 RIR; ≤4 = effort rating only. RPE and RIR interchangeable at RPE ≥5.
- Load prescription: use bands, not exact targets (e.g., 4×8 @6–8 RPE). RPE-selected loads beat fixed % over 8–12 weeks (Helms 2018; Graham & Cleather 2019) because average intensity drifts higher; reps achievable @70% 1RM squat vary 6–28 (avg 16±4) and 6–26 (14±4) between lifters.
- 4-mesocycle example (each 4–6 wk): Meso1 3×10/4×8/5×6 @5–7; Meso2 3×8/4×6/5×4 @6–8; Meso3 3×6/4×4/4×2 @7–9; Meso4 3×5/3×3/single @8–9.5 (single @9.5). Accessories: same format but keep reps higher (no singles/triples on isolation).
- Missed-RPE adjustment chart (Helms dissertation; target band 6–8): actual RPE 1 → +20%; 2 → +16%; 3 → +12%; 4 → +8%; 5 → +4%; 6–8 → lifter's choice; 8.5 → −2%; 9 → −4%; 9.5 → −6%; 10 → −8%. Rule of thumb: ~2% load change per 0.5 RPE outside the band.
- RPE stops (volume autoregulation): (1) per-set — fixed load (e.g., 70%), stop each set at target RPE: ~7–8 RPE for volume blocks; ~9 RPE when doing 2–4 reps @85–90%; (2) per-session — fixed reps (8) @70%, add sets until session RPE cap (8.5), hard cap 5 sets (volume) or 3 sets (intensity block). Built-in progression: hold load until all target sets/reps land under the RPE cap, then raise load. Preferred over velocity-loss thresholds because first-rep velocity decays across sets, so a fixed % velocity loss drifts closer to failure by set 4.
- Total-rep / rest-pause (assistance): load ≈ 12–15RM; each set to 8–9 RPE; accumulate total-rep threshold (e.g., 35 reps: 11+9+8+7). Rest-pause variant: 20–30 s between sets; less next-48h fatigue than failure training. Progress when threshold reached in fewer sets.
- Tracking / daily-1RM prediction: 1 rep @9 RPE ≈ 95% → daily 1RM = load/0.95 (150 kg @9 → ~157.5 kg). Progress check: same 150 kg single drops from 9 RPE to 7 RPE → projected 1RM ~172.5 kg. Volume progress: 3×8@105 kg ≤8 RPE → weeks later 6×8@105 kg, last set @7. Avoids fatiguing 1RM tests (which force deload/extended intro week after each block).
- Progression via RPE: inverse relationship — lower RPE, bigger load bump; repeat a fixed prescription until average RPE falls (e.g., 3×10 @ avg 9 → repeat until avg 8 → add load or a set; build to 5×10 @ avg 8, then reset to 3×10 with +2.5 kg).
- Warm-up readiness triage (flexible templates): decide session type after warming to 80–85% 1RM and rating RPE (person's normal @85% assumed 6): ≥8 → light session; 5–7 → moderate; <5 → heavy. Pre-workout recovery Likert (1–10) correlates poorly with performance; warm-up RPE is the better indicator.
- Accuracy limits: low-rep/heavy sets — strikingly accurate in trained lifters (no evidence velocity beats it there). High-rep @70%: intra-set calls at 5/7/9 RPE averaged 5.15±2.92 reps off, errors over-rated (more true RIR than believed — safer direction). Self-validation drill: mid-set RPE call, then continue to failure, using ≥80% 1RM at expected ~7 RPE. Risk case: autoregulating heavy singles — mis-rated 8 (actually 9) could prompt overreaching attempt; coach fix: prescribe a load known to be an 8 RPE single with optional second rep to prove failure doesn't occur.
- Evidence: validation study (Zourdos 2016) + RCTs (Helms 2018; Graham & Cleather 2019) + dissertation (missed-RPE chart). Nuzzo 2024 reps-at-%1RM spread is via PYR.

## Corpus-internal flags

- "VBT ~50% larger strength gains": article flags magnitude as inflated (6 weeks, feedback confound); treat directionally.
- "70% of strength variance explained by muscle size" (Trezise 2016): single knee-extensor prediction study; hedged in text.
- Periodized > non-periodized: article concedes specificity-artifact criticism (post-test timing).
- Willoughby 1993: 30-year-old, small-sample; illustrative classic.
- Daily/near-daily maxing: effective short-term, unproven long-term.
- RPE/RIR usage: no conflicts — the RIR-based scale remains the standard the corpus helped establish.
